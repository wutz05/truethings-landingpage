You know their favourite pizza. But do you know what they wanted to be as a kid, which app they open first in the morning and what they're secretly afraid of? How Well Do You Know Me is the simplest couples quiz there is: one asks, the other guesses. Here are 100 questions in eight categories, plus rules, scoring and versions for the couch, for texting and for video calls.

How to play How Well Do You Know Me

You need two pieces of paper, two pens and fifteen minutes.

  1. Ask. One of you reads out a question about themselves, for example: “What do I almost always order at a restaurant?”
  2. Both write. The one who asked writes down their real answer. The other writes down a guess.
  3. Reveal together. A correct guess scores a point. If it's close, the person the question was about decides.
  4. Swap. Take turns after every question. After ten questions each you know who knows whom better.

Writing it down sounds fussy, but it matters: nobody can adjust their answer afterwards, and the reveal is the best moment of the game.

Play for stakes: the loser cooks tomorrow, takes out the bins for a week or plans the next date, alone and as a surprise.

Why we know each other less than we think

The longer you're together, the surer you are that you know each other inside out. That's the trap. In a study by Benjamin Scheibehenne, Jutta Mata and Peter Todd, couples predicted their partner's preferences, including food and films. Young couples got it right more often than couples who had been together for decades. The older couples were still more confident in their guesses.

One likely reason: early on you ask a lot, later you assume you already know. But people change. The favourite meal from five years ago isn't necessarily the favourite meal now.

A quiz is the playful way to refresh what you know. Wrong answers are the interesting part: behind every “wait, since when?” there's a story you haven't heard yet.

Five ways to change it up

By text

One of you sends the question, both type an answer and hit send on three. Send too early and you lose the point. Works on a lunch break and long distance.

Lightning round

Sixty seconds, out loud only: one fires questions, the other guesses straight away. Count the correct answers, then swap. The daily life and food categories work best for this.

Number guesses

Anything with numbers: screen time, unread emails, pairs of shoes, ideal hours of sleep. Closest guess takes the point, and the phone settles the argument.

Double date

Two couples, one host: one person from each couple leaves the room while the other answers five questions. Then the partners come back and have to match those answers. This is how the game works at weddings and birthdays too.

In reverse

Instead of “what's my favourite film?”, ask “what do I think your favourite film is?”. So your partner has to guess what you believe about them. It gets absurd quickly and shows which assumptions are long out of date.

When the guess is wrong

Sooner or later one of you gets a question completely wrong. Three things turn that into a good moment:

  • Ask “since when?”. Often the answer used to be right. Preferences change more quietly than you'd think.
  • Tell the story. “A vet” is an answer. Why you wanted to be a vet at eight is a conversation.
  • Count points, not love. Not knowing the vegetable doesn't mean loving less. It means not having asked in a while.
