You don't fight. You like each other. And still every evening feels like the one before: dinner, a show, phones, bed. Being bored in a relationship doesn't mean the love is gone. Most of the time it only means nothing new has happened for a while. Here's what research says about it, and twelve things you can do, from ten minutes to a whole weekend.

Is it normal to be bored in a relationship?

Yes. What was exciting at the start becomes familiar, and familiar is a good thing at first: you know each other, you rely on each other and you have nothing left to prove. But calm and boredom aren't the same.

If the right-hand column sounds like you, there's no need to panic. It is a reason to do something, though.

Why boredom isn't harmless

Irene Tsapelas, Arthur Aron and Terri Orbuch interviewed 123 married couples in year seven and again in year sixteen of their marriage. Couples who reported more boredom in year seven were clearly less satisfied nine years later. It didn't work the other way round: being dissatisfied in year seven didn't predict being bored later.

The path ran through closeness. Boredom went with less closeness, and less closeness with less satisfaction. The study shows a link over time, not proof of cause and effect. But it's a good argument for taking boredom seriously: it's no drama, yet it works quietly.

Where the boredom comes from

Weeks that look the same disappear

Routine is efficient, but it leaves few memories. In experiments by Dinah Avni-Babad and Ilana Ritov, people remembered stretches of time as shorter when they were filled with routine. Applied to couples: three months of identical weeks feel like a single week afterwards.

You stop asking

At the start you wanted to know everything about each other. At some point you assume you know the answers and stop asking. Yet couples often know each other less well than they think. You can test that with our 100 How Well Do You Know Me questions.

The trap: bored couples do less

You might expect boredom to push couples towards new ideas all by itself. A diary study of 122 couples over 21 days found the opposite: the more bored partners were, the less often they did something exciting together, and the less they enjoyed their shared activities. So boredom doesn't fix itself. You have to plan the first step, especially when you don't feel like it.

What helps: new beats nice

The best-known experiment on this comes from the psychologist Arthur Aron. Couples were given a task for seven minutes. Some were strapped together at the wrist and ankle with Velcro and had to crawl across gym mats together, holding a pillow between them. The others got a calm task with a ball. Afterwards the Velcro couples rated their relationship higher than before, and the gain was bigger than in the calm group.

In an earlier study, 53 married couples spent an hour and a half a week for ten weeks on activities. One group did “exciting” things, the other “pleasant” ones. At the end the exciting group was more satisfied with their marriage.

The point isn't the Velcro. An evening at your favourite restaurant is nice, but it isn't new. What helps against boredom is something unfamiliar that challenges you a little and that you do together.

12 things to do when you're bored in your relationship

Sorted by effort. Start small: one idea from the first group is enough for tonight.

In ten minutes

1. Ask a question you've never asked each other. Not “how was work?”, but for example: “What do you want more of in your days? Not your life, just your days.” You'll find a hundred of these in our deck of deep questions for couples.

2. Play a round against each other. A little competition wakes you up: whoever loses wants a rematch, and suddenly you're teasing each other again. Cards, four in a row or a quiz, as long as it's short.

3. Send something that organises nothing. Scroll through your chat: how many of the last 50 messages are about the two of you, and how many about groceries, appointments and parcels? Send one today that only says: thinking of you. A drawing counts double.

4. Guess instead of asking. “What do you think I'd most like to eat right now?” Guessing shows where you overestimate yourselves, and it nearly always ends in a story.

In one evening

5. Cook something neither of you has ever cooked. Pick a country you've both never been to and cook a dish from there. The chance that it goes wrong is part of it: you're both beginners.

6. Swap roles. Whoever usually plans gets surprised. Whoever usually tags along organises the evening alone and reveals nothing but the time and the dress code.

7. Go on a colour hunt. One of you names a colour, and you both have 30 seconds to find something in exactly that shade and take a photo of it. Closest match wins. It works at home, in the supermarket and on a walk.

8. Guess your old photos. Each of you picks four photos from your time together, and the other has to say when and where it was. You'll be amazed how much you've forgotten, and how much of it is worth doing again.

9. Be tourists in your own town. Ride a bus or train line you never take to the last stop and walk back from there. Or eat at the place you've been walking past for years.

Over a weekend and beyond

10. Learn something neither of you can do. A dance class, bouldering, pottery or a language for your next trip. What matters is that nobody has a head start.

11. Plan a micro-adventure. Sleep outside for a night, climb a hill for sunrise, take a local train to a town you only know from the timetable. Small, cheap, and you'll still be talking about it in a year.

12. Give new things a fixed slot. An hour and a half a week was enough in the study above. Put it in the calendar like a doctor's appointment and take turns choosing. Without a slot, the couch wins.

The rule for all twelve: new, together, slightly unfamiliar. If an idea feels a bit silly, that's usually a good sign.

Day to day: respond to the small bids

Adventures aren't everything. The relationship researcher John Gottman observed newlywed couples and checked six years later who was still together. The small moments made the difference: a “look at that bird”, a question, a sigh. Couples who had stayed together had responded to these little bids almost nine times out of ten. For the couples who later divorced, it was only about one time in three.

For boredom that means: put the phone down when your partner shows you something. Ask a follow-up when they tell you something. It takes ten seconds, and everything else builds on it.

When boredom means more

Not every lull is just routine. Talk openly with each other if

  • you've tried new ideas and nothing changes for weeks,
  • one of you doesn't want to change anything at all,
  • boredom has turned into indifference or irritation,
  • you'd rather avoid each other than spend time together.

In that case an honest conversation helps more than any date idea, and sometimes couples counselling does. “I'm bored with us” hurts. “I miss doing new things with you” says the same and invites the other person in.

The takeaway: nobody did anything wrong

Boredom in a relationship shows up when nothing new has happened for a long time. Nobody did anything wrong. Nobody did anything, that's all. Pick one of the twelve ideas, ideally one for tonight. Seven minutes are enough to start.
